Case Study

How Bloom Store replaced a 6-month dev backlog with one weekend in Stackly

Sarah KimOperations Lead, Bloom Store · Aug 12, 2026 · 8 min read

Bloom Store is a three-location plant and homeware retailer with an online shop that grew faster than anyone planned. By January, the ops team was juggling order tracking in one spreadsheet, inventory in another, and wholesale requests over email. The agency quote to build a proper internal system: six months, $80,000. This is the story of how they shipped it in a weekend instead — told by the person who built it.

The backlog that wouldn't move

Every retailer knows this shape of problem. The store runs on Shopify, but everything around the store — purchase orders, supplier lead times, wholesale approvals, the repotting service calendar — lives in tools that were never meant to talk to each other. Our "system" was four spreadsheets, a shared inbox, and me, manually reconciling all of it every Sunday night.

We scoped a custom build twice. Both times the timeline landed somewhere between "two quarters" and "don't ask." Meanwhile the team was losing roughly ten hours a week to copy-paste work, and wholesale orders were slipping through the cracks — we later measured about 6% of them simply never got a reply.

Friday, 7 PM: picking a starting point

I'd seen Stackly's template marketplace in a community thread, so I opened the "Retail Ops" starter — pages for orders, inventory, suppliers, and a customer portal, with the data model already wired. My honest expectation was a toy. Twenty minutes in, I had renamed the collections to match our world (Plants, Planters, Suppliers, Wholesale Accounts) and dropped our logo and colors into the theme panel.

"I kept waiting for the moment where the tool would say 'now hand this to a developer.' It never came. The wall I braced for just wasn't there."
— Sarah Kim, Operations Lead at Bloom Store

By the time I closed the laptop on Friday, the app had our five core pages sketched out and the nav made sense. Total time invested: about two hours, one of which was me fiddling with fonts because I could.

Saturday: modeling data like it actually works

Saturday was the real test: could the built-in database handle our actual complexity? Our inventory isn't flat — a single plant SKU varies by pot size, and wholesale pricing depends on the account tier. In Stackly that became three related collections with lookup fields, built entirely in the visual schema editor. Relations are drawn, not coded.

The part that surprised me most was permissions. Store managers see their own location's stock; the wholesale team sees accounts and pricing; our two retail partners get a portal view with only their orders. That's four roles, configured from a dropdown, with protected pages out of the box.

Sunday: workflows that run the store

Sunday morning I opened the workflow canvas and automated the Sunday-night job that used to be mine:

  • Low-stock alerts — when any variant drops below its reorder point, Slack pings the right location's channel with a one-click purchase-order draft.
  • Wholesale approvals — new applications route to the wholesale lead; approval triggers a welcome email and unlocks tier pricing automatically.
  • Order status sync — a webhook from Shopify updates the order record, so the portal always shows live status without anyone touching it.
  • Weekly digest — every Monday at 8 AM, managers get an email with sales, stock-outs, and pending approvals for their location.

Each of those is an if-this-then-that chain on the canvas. The Shopify piece uses the built-in webhook trigger — the entire configuration is a URL and a field mapping:

workflow: order-status-sync
// Trigger
WHEN  webhook "shopify/orders/updated" receives payload

// Steps
FIND  record in Orders where shopify_id = payload.id
SET   status     = payload.fulfillment_status
SET   updated_at = now()
IF    status = "delivered"
  SEND email "review-request" to record.customer

Monday: launch, and the part nobody believed

Monday at 9 AM I hit Publish. SSL, custom domain (ops.bloomstore.co), and role-based logins for eleven people — live before the first coffee refill. The team's reaction went from polite skepticism to "wait, can you add a field for pot color?" in about an hour. (I added it during the meeting. That's the whole point.)

Six weeks later, the numbers are holding: wholesale response time dropped from days to hours, stock-outs on our top 20 SKUs are down 40%, and the Sunday-night spreadsheet ritual is gone. When something needs to change, we change it in the builder — usually in minutes, usually while the person who asked is still standing there.

What I'd tell anyone staring at a dev backlog

Start with a template that's 70% right rather than a blank canvas, model your data before you polish pixels, and automate the task you personally hate most first — the momentum from deleting a recurring chore carries the whole project. And publish early: a live app with three working pages beats a perfect spec every time.

The backlog didn't shrink — it evaporated. If your team is sitting on one, the four-step process we followed is documented end to end, and the Retail Ops template we started from is in the gallery.
